Trees and forests

City woods offer twice the cooling of larger parks, Leeds study finds

Two summers of sensor data suggest woodland beats parks for escaping city heat

Fri 6 Nov 2026 09.00 GMT

Share

When the heat builds in a British city, many people head for the park. A study in Leeds suggests they should go a little further – into the woods.

Over two summers, patches of urban woodland in Leeds were on average 1.4C cooler than built-up streets – twice the cooling of the city’s larger parks, the researchers found.

The team hung sensors from branches in woods, parks and allotments across the city, and fixed others to street furniture in built-up areas. Every 15 minutes through the summers of 2023 and 2024, each one logged the temperature and humidity of the air around it.

Small city-centre parks were on average just 0.4C cooler than the streets around them, and larger parks 0.7C. In the woods, the gap at its widest passed 4C in the early afternoon.

Jim Parker, a researcher at Leeds Beckett University, led the work with colleagues at the University of Leeds. It is published in the journal People and Nature. Their 11 wooded sites included Gledhow Valley Wood and Middleton Wood.

The findings come after a record-breaking summer. In September the Met Office said summer 2026 was provisionally the UK’s hottest since records began in 1884.

The UK Health Security Agency’s interim estimate links the May and June heatwaves to 2,877 excess deaths in England, almost double its figure for the whole of summer 2025.

“This summer has seen records smashed across the UK, with four heatwaves already on the books this year,” said Jim McQuaid, a University of Leeds co-author, in July.

Trees cool their surroundings in several ways, according to Forest Research, the government’s tree science agency. Their canopy blocks sunlight before it heats the ground.

Their leaves release water vapour – a process that draws heat from the air. And trees store less heat than concrete and tarmac.

Air temperature is only part of how heat feels. The researchers also used a “feels like” measure, the Universal Thermal Climate Index, which adds sunshine, wind and humidity. With it, they counted the hours of strong heat stress – when it feels like 32C to 38C.

In summer 2023, by their most cautious calculation, the woods had about two-thirds fewer of those hours than the streets.

Allowing for shade from the canopy raised the estimate to 96%. But that figure rests on assumptions. Sunshine and wind came from regional weather data rather than local sensors, and the shading values were borrowed from a study of a forest in China.

Woodland also spent far less time above a temperature linked to rising deaths. The paper uses 22.2C as a danger line, drawing on earlier research that found heat-related deaths across Yorkshire begin to rise once daytime highs pass about that level. Over both summers, woodland sites spent 63% fewer hours above that temperature than built-up streets.

These aren’t just nice-to-have, they are critical infrastructure

Cat Scott, University of Leeds

Cat Scott, a University of Leeds co-author, said trees and green spaces should be taken more seriously. “These aren’t just nice-to-have, they are critical infrastructure,” she said in a university statement.

For many people, home offers little relief. About one in eight households in Great Britain had no access to a private or shared garden, the Office for National Statistics said in 2020.

Air conditioning is rare. Researchers at the University of Reading found that only 4.3% of English homes use it, and just 2.5% of the lowest-earning fifth.

“The UK is heading towards a cooling divide,” said Rory Jones, who led the Reading study and was not involved in the Leeds research.

Shade is not shared out evenly within Leeds either. A survey by University of Leeds researchers, published in 2020, found tree canopy covered about 30% of Roundhay, where two of the study’s woods stand. In Beeston and Holbeck it was less than 10%.

Across the UK, towns and cities average 17.9% tree cover, against about 30% in Europe. That comes from an analysis of 2018 satellite data by the Energy and Climate Intelligence Unit, which measured cover differently from the Leeds survey. Greater Manchester came out as the greenest of Britain’s big urban areas, at 27%.

“The lack of urban trees in the UK is stark,” said Kate Sheldon, the chief executive of the charity Trees for Cities.

The Leeds study has limits. It covered one city over two summers, with between one and three sensors in each wood. The team did not measure how much of each site was under canopy, so it cannot say how many trees a street would need for the same effect. Some parks were wooded too, so the categories overlap, and the paper compares averages rather than testing the differences statistically.

Overnight, the gap between green spaces and streets largely closed. And the study measured the air, not people: it did not track anyone’s health.

The authors call for cities to protect the woods they have, plant more, and make sure those who most need cool spaces can reach them. Leeds city council has set a target of raising tree cover from 17% to 33% by 2050.

But two national grant schemes for planting trees in towns and on council land have closed. Government guidance pages had marked the Urban Tree Challenge Fund and the Local Authority Treescapes Fund closed by summer 2024, and they name no replacement.

Some planting is in residents’ hands. Leeds households can sponsor a street tree through Trees for Streets, which Trees for Cities runs with the council. Planting takes place between November and April.

When it launched in 2023, sponsors were asked for £150 a tree, and local Liberal Democrats called it a “leafy suburb subsidy”. “I can’t see there being many customers in the city’s council estates that have a spare £150 to spend on a tree,” said Conrad Hart-Brooke, a Liberal Democrat councillor, at the time.

Mohammed Rafique, then the council’s executive member for environment, replied that communities, schools and clubs could share the cost. “Every extra tree that is planted is beneficial to us all,” he said.

Parker said planting and protecting trees was “a practical and effective way to make cities safer”, as well as healthier and more comfortable as the climate warms.
