Plastics

Bottled water samples hold about 240,000 plastic particles a litre, many likely from the bottle and its filters, study estimates

Most were nanoplastics, too small for earlier methods to see, and the researchers suspect much of the plastic came from the bottle and its filters. The 2024 study of three US brands did not test health effects.

Fri 6 Nov 2026 09.00 GMT

Share

A sealed bottle of water sells a simple promise: whatever is in the tap, this is cleaner. Its label names a spring or a mountain, not what else might be inside. In a 2024 study, samples from three brands averaged 240,000 identified particles of plastic per litre, most of which are too tiny to see. The researchers think much of the plastic comes from the bottle and its filters.

In the study, published in the January 2024 issue of the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, Naixin Qian from Columbia University and colleagues found that roughly nine in ten of the identified particles were nanoplastics by number, the particles that are smaller than one micrometre, which is one-thousandth of a millimetre.

Previous research focused on discovering larger pieces, hence the smaller ones were missed. This is why the count is so much higher than earlier studies reported. The water has not necessarily become dirtier. The tests became more precise.

Qian told the Associated Press that much of the plastic contamination appears to stem from the bottles and the reverse osmosis membranes used for purification. In other words, the packaging and purification that justify the price could themselves be the sources. The experiment did not trace individual particles, so this is the researchers’ reading, not a proven chain.

The combinations of plastics indicate the same thing. Among the three brands, the most common type of counted plastic was polyamide, a type of nylon, which the authors said probably originates from purification filters. PET (polyethylene terephthalate) used for the production of bottles is also present, and in one of the brands it is reported to account for around 63% of the estimated weight. Columbia’s announcement of the study said PET probably gets into the water as bits slough off when the bottle is squeezed or exposed to heat.

Plastic in what we eat is not news. According to a 2019 study reported by the Guardian, it is estimated that we ingest at least 50,000 pieces of plastic in a year, and bottled water is one of the major contributors. What is new, however, is being able to measure the smallest particles, which concern scientists most.

Zoie Diana, a University of Toronto researcher who was not involved, told the AP that such particles “can appear in different organs and may cross membranes that they aren’t meant to cross, such as the blood-brain barrier”.

The research does not prove that bottled water is better than tap water. The research conducted by the team at Columbia University did not involve testing tap water. Meanwhile, a separate study at Ohio State University revealed in February 2026 that the quantity of nanoplastics present in six brands of bottled water was three times higher than in treated tap water near Lake Erie, although the methodology was different.

Much of the plastic seems to be coming from the bottle itself and the reverse osmosis membrane filter

Naixin Qian, lead author, to the Associated Press

In order to tally such tiny particles, a method was required that not only recognised each of them but also classified them as plastic. It was important because the term nanoplastic refers to the size of a particle, not its composition.

“It is one thing to detect, but another to know what you are detecting,” said Wei Min, a co-author who developed the technology.

Using stimulated Raman scattering microscopy, the group was able to detect chemical compounds present in the sample. Two lasers made the chemical bonds vibrate, and different types of plastic had different vibrational patterns which made it possible to match each particle against seven common plastics.

Yet, only around one in ten of the tiny items that the microscope detected matched any of the seven. The others, which could be other plastics or something else entirely, were not counted.

In experiments, the technique was able to detect plastic spheres that measure approximately 100 nanometres. A strand of human hair is generally around 80 to 100 micrometres wide, which means that about 800 of these spheres can fit across that strand.

This is the reason that the research is significant beyond three bottles. Anything that cannot be quantified cannot be tracked. In July, the US Environmental Protection Agency proposed excluding microplastics from drinking-water testing, stating that “there is no validated EPA or consensus drinking water analytical method”.

The situation in Britain is no better. The legal list of what English water companies must assess covers a wide array of substances, but it does not mention plastic. The BSI, the standards organisation, released guidelines on how to sample water for microplastics only in 2025. Until a method is validated, no regulator on either side of the Atlantic can tell anyone how much plastic is present in the water.

The AP reported that Kara Lavender Law, an oceanography researcher at the Sea Education Association who was not part of the study, said the work “can be an important advance in the detection of nanoplastics”. However, she thought other chemists should reproduce it before anyone builds rules on it.

The method has been contested already. In a critique released in the same journal in November 2024, Dušan Materić stated that contamination testing by the researchers did not cover the whole process. Qian and colleagues responded that the tests were appropriate. The debate is not settled yet.

The study has three limits. First, it did not test how the particles affect people who drink them, so it shows neither harm nor safety. “We don’t know if it’s dangerous or how dangerous,” said Phoebe Stapleton, an expert in toxicology and study co-author at Rutgers University.

Second, its top number is an estimate extrapolated from small patches of filters, and it is a count rather than a weight. A thousand nanoplastics could weigh less than one piece that is twenty times wider. Third, it included only three US brands sold by one retailer, so it says nothing about the bottle in the hand of a UK reader.

What it does show is enough. An unopened bottle does not ensure that the water is free of plastic, the bottle and its filters are the likely sources, and no regulator is yet checking how much plastic the water contains.

“This opens a window where we can look into a world that was not exposed to us before,” said Beizhan Yan, a co-author and environmental chemist from Columbia’s Lamont-Doherty Earth Observatory.
